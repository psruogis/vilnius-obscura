What the Cathedral Square–Pilies–Didžioji–Town Hall–Aušros Vartų spine looked like around 1794. I reused the earlier run's transcript and added about 60 new searches and fetches.

**The city in general.** In 1794 Vilnius was a late-baroque city rebuilt after the fires of 1737, 1748 and 1749, largely by Johann Christoph Glaubitz. The main streets were almost entirely masonry. The 1808 plan shows less than 10% wooden buildings. Many houses stood empty or ruined: 35 empty properties out of about 300 in the magistrate's jurisdiction in 1790. The second big building boom came only in the 19th century.

**Where "today's building, back-dated" breaks down.** Five places need their own treatment:
- **Cathedral Square did not exist.** The Cathedral was hemmed in by:
  - the three-storey Castle Gate, rebuilt 1785, with a guard post from 1794 and demolished 1837;
  - the Supreme Tribunal building, which got an archive wing in 1790 and was demolished 1836–37;
  - the old Bishops' house, the chapter and vicars' houses, arsenals and the Lower Castle walls;
  - the ruined Palace of the Grand Dukes, demolished 1799–1801.
- **Ground level was about 1.5 m lower** at the Cathedral than today.
- **All ten city gates and the wall still stood.** They were demolished 1799–1804; the Castle Gate went in 1837.
- **The two great neoclassical projects were unfinished building sites.**
  - Cathedral: work halted in 1792 and resumed in 1798, finished 1801. It had no roof statues in 1794: a Smuglewicz sketch from 1797 shows none, and Peszka's 1808 drawing shows them.
  - Town Hall: built 1785/88–1799 and halted during 1793–95. A new loan came in 1797.
- **Several spine buildings date from after 1794:**
  - Pilies 8 façade (1800), Didžioji 1 façade (1824), Chodkiewicz palace remodel (1825–34);
  - Presidential Palace (1824–34);
  - St Nicholas and St Paraskeva on Didžioji, both rebuilt 1865;
  - St Casimir's onion domes (1864–68), which were later removed; its crown cupola dates from about 1755;
  - St Anne's bell tower (1872–73);
  - the Gates of Dawn chapel's classicist façade (1829);
  - the Philharmonic (1899–1902), on the site of the merchants' guest house;
  - many storeys added after 1900.

**Street life.**
- **Paving:** stone paving from the 15th–16th centuries, drainage channels on Pilies by 1642, and a paving toll in 1791.
- **Water:** wooden pipes running downhill from springs to public cisterns called rurmusai, at the Town Hall Square, the Lower Castle, Vokiečių and St Johns'.
- **Lighting:** public lanterns in 1794 are doubtful at best. Sources conflict. Oil lamps were documented from 1824 and gas from 1864.
- **People:** about 17,500 in 1796, roughly 40% Jewish around 1800. A 1742 privilege let Jews live and trade anywhere except the procession route from the Gates of Dawn to the Cathedral – the spine itself.
- **The Vilna Gaon** was alive in 1794, living in a house on Žydų street.

**Back-dating rules.**
- Remove 19th/20th-century added storeys, mansard roofs and historicist façades.
- Roofs were mostly clay tile at about 45°. Metal roofs were only for churches and the rich.
- Baroque colour schemes were vivid (red ochre walls with white trim and grey reliefs) and were whitewashed later.

**Visual sources.**
- Smuglewicz's 1785–86 cycle: 21 originals at the National Museum in Kraków; some are public domain on Wikimedia Commons. A 1912 album reproducing 23 plates is also public domain online.
- Town plans: Getkant 1648; the so-called 1737 plan in the Fürstenhoff collection (Berlin State Library); a 1787 "French" plan of the castle area; Russian army plans of 1793, 1794, 1798 and 1799 in the Russian military archive in Moscow (fond 846); the 1808 plan.
- Georeferenced data: VilniausDNR reconstructs the 1808 map, and the Lithuanian Institute of History has a GIS of property plots.
- Later sources: Wilczyński's Album Wileńskie (public domain, on Polona) and Czechowicz's 1870s photographs.

**Still unresolved:** the exact state in 1794 of the Cathedral, the Town Hall, the Gates of Dawn gallery and the Cathedral belfry's top; whether there were street lanterns; per-building storey counts and colours.
- **The three great fires: 2 June 1737, 11 June 1748, 8 June 1749 burned most of the walled city including large parts of the spine** [secondary-source]
  1737: started near the Royal windmill at the Vilnia bridge (Užupis); half to three-quarters of the city burned (sources vary); St Johns' church, its bell tower and university buildings destroyed. 1748: started 9 a.m. in the Jewish brewer Rubin's brewery in Užupis; 469 brick buildings, 12 churches, 15 palaces, 142 shops, 4 pharmacies, 29 dead; St Johns', the Jesuit college/university, Franciscan and Dominican churches, synagogues and the Bishops' Palace burned. 1749: a chimney spark near Subačius Gate; 292 brick houses; Vokiečių, Arklių, Rūdninkų, Žemaitijos, Trakų, Stiklių and Žydų streets; St Casimir's (for the third time), the Dominican church and synagogues burned. The Town Hall burned in 1748/1749 and the wooden Holy Spirit church in 1749. St Theresa's burned in a separate fire in 1760. Art bible: in 1794 most spine façades are 30–55-year-old late-baroque rebuilds on Gothic/Renaissance cellars and walls.

- **The post-fire late-baroque ('Vilnian Baroque') rebuild was led by Johann Christoph Glaubitz (c.1700–1767, in Vilnius from 1738) plus a few other architects** [secondary-source]
  Glaubitz: St Johns' façade and the upper two baroque tiers and helmet of its belfry (1738–1749); St Catherine's; renovation of the Cathedral belfry and façade towers; the Town Hall rebuild after 1749 (tower finished 1769, with S. Russel directing after 1756); Holy Spirit Orthodox church (1749–1753; iconostasis 1756–57); the Basilian Gate (1761, 17.9 m); St Theresa's after the 1760 fire; repairs to St Anne's (1747). Others: Tomasz Żebrowski (St Casimir's crown cupola, finished by 1755); Abraham Würtzner and Franciszek Ignacy Hoffer (Chodkiewicz palace, Didžioji 4, 1754–62). Style markers for the art bible: pairs of slender towers, undulating and broken pediments and cornices, rhythmic pilasters, wrought-iron openwork crosses and finials, stucco and artificial marble inside. en.wikipedia also credits Glaubitz with the mid-18th-century interior of the Great Synagogue (unverified elsewhere).

- **1794 Vilnius was overwhelmingly masonry on the main streets but full of empty or ruined houses; the second building boom only came in the 19th century** [confirmed-reliable]
  From the Lithuanian Institute of History study of Old Town property plots (M. Klovas, E. Meilus, O. Valionienė, 'Vilniaus senamiesčio posesijų raidos XVI–XVIII a. apžvalga'). The 1636 revision counted 717 plots inside the walls, 450 of them masonry. Pilies, Didžioji, Vokiečių, Dominikonų and Šv. Jono were built almost entirely in masonry. Wooden houses were concentrated along the wall, in the western Jewish area, in lanes and north near the Cathedral. On the 1808 plan wooden buildings are about 10% or less, mostly around St Nicholas in the south-east. In the 18th century the magistrate's jurisdiction had about 300 plots. Empty plots numbered 45 (1717), 64 (1740, 23%), 50 (1750) and 35 each in 1760, 1768 and 1790. The authors: the city was 'full of empty or ruined masonry houses, slowly rebuilt'. Art bible: include a few gutted shells and vacant plots even on the spine.

- **Cathedral in 1794: Gucevičius's neoclassical rebuild was a stalled building site; the three roof statues were almost certainly NOT yet there** [secondary-source]
  2 Nov 1769: a storm toppled the south-west tower, killing 6. Gucevičius's design dates from 1781; preparation began in 1782 and construction in 1783, under Bishop Ignacy Massalski; scaffolding went up inside and out from December 1785. Tommaso Righi (in Vilnius c.1785–91) carved the Evangelists in the portico niches and the Noah's-sacrifice relief in the pediment. Work was cancelled in 1792 over Massalski's financial crisis. Until 1798 the interior was essentially unchanged apart from new vaults and new chapel masonry. Work resumed in spring 1798 under Mykolas Šulcas (Gucevičius died 1798) and finished with the consecration in 1801. Statues: VLE dates Jelskis's St Helena, St Casimir and St Stanislaus to 1792, but three sources point to later: V. Drėma found chapter documents of three large WOODEN statues on the pediment in 1799 (possibly mock-ups); Smuglewicz's 1797 sketch (MNK Kraków) shows none; Józef Peszka's 1808 drawing (Vilnius University Library) shows them. The present copper statues are by S. Kuzma (1996/97); originals removed 1950. The 12 statues on the side façades came only after 1831. Back-dating: no roof statues, no side-façade saints, an unfinished interior, probably with scaffolding and building debris. The exterior shell and six-column Doric portico were probably standing (Smuglewicz 1797), but completion in 1794 is not confirmed.

- **Cathedral bell tower in 1794 had a baroque top; its present classicist upper stage, clock dials and obelisk are Šulcas's post-1798 work** [secondary-source]
  The bell tower stands on a square castle tower (14th c.) and a round defensive tower with three rows of gun-port-like windows. Above that rise octagonal bell tiers (16th c.; third tier completed 1598). After the 1610 fire Wilhelm Pohl rebuilt it and bells and a clock were installed; a mechanical clock is documented from the mid-17th century. Glaubitz renovated it in the mid-18th century. It had baroque features in the 18th century, but the exact form is unknown: the only depiction is a Smuglewicz panorama showing a round baroque cupola from afar. Marceli Januszkiewicz's 1840 watercolour is anachronistic. Šulcas's reconstruction (early 19th c., 'nearly 20 years') enlarged the top stage, added four clock dials and an obelisk above the dome, and put early-classical stucco on the lower stages. The iron cross on a gilded drum is from 1893. Height today is 52 m (57 m with cross). Back-dating: strip the clock-stage classicist decoration, dials and obelisk; give it a baroque domed cap after the Smuglewicz panorama.

- **There was no open Cathedral Square in 1794: the Cathedral was hemmed in by Lower Castle buildings, the Castle Gate, the Tribunal and clergy houses** [secondary-source]
  Castle Gate (Pilies vartai), at the head of Pilies street: rebuilt 1785 with a third storey; held the Tribunal archive and castle-court chancellery, with a nobles' prison below. A guard post was set up there in 1794. It measured 23.5 × 10 × 13.8 m, with a baroque portal of dark decorated panels and a white-stone cartouche bearing the white eagle and the Vasa sheaf. Demolished 1837; four granite blocks mark the site. Supreme Tribunal building: archive wing added 1790 to Marcin Knackfus's design, costing 46,915 złoty; demolished 1836–37 together with the chapter house, vicars' houses and old Bishops' house. The area south of the Cathedral was then levelled and paved. Old Bishops' house south-east of the bell tower: from 1720 a 'Collegium dioecesanum Invalidorum' for aged priests; rebuilt mid-18th c.; three storeys, 58 × 11 m, with two turrets at the west end. Palace of the Grand Dukes: abandoned for about 150 years after 1655–61, with families living in the ruined parts in the late 1700s; demolished 1799–1801 and the bricks sold; a fragment survives in Abraham Schlossberg's house. In 1750 the Prussian visitor L. von Werner found the castle buildings abandoned and crumbling, except the Tribunal, the basilica and the arsenal. The left branch of the Vilnia around the Lower Castle was channelled from the mid-19th c. and eliminated by 1882. Archaeology found a 17th–18th-c. paved square about 1.5 m below today's surface. Iconography: J. Oziębłowski's 1835 lithograph of the Castle Gate and Tribunal; Smuglewicz's 'Lower Castle' (1785, Wikimedia Commons, public domain).

- **The Upper Castle and Gediminas Tower in 1794 were a roofless ruin, with the western tower standing four storeys high** [secondary-source]
  The Upper Castle had lost its defensive role by the early 17th century. The siege of 1655–61 damaged it, including destroying the western tower's roof, and it was never repaired. In 1787 Marcin Knackfus made measured drawings and talked the authorities out of demolishing it. The western tower had four storeys (square base, octagonal upper part) until 1837–38, when Russian authorities removed the two upper storeys and put a wooden two-storey hexagonal optical-telegraph structure on top (it operated until 1854). The 1930s restoration removed the telegraph, rebuilt the third storey and added a viewing platform; Jan Borowski is usually named as the architect (not confirmed here). Backdrop: a jagged four-storey roofless tower beside the ruins of the palace on the hill.

- **The Town Hall in 1794 was an unfinished Gucevičius building site, and the square was the main market and execution place** [secondary-source]
  The old Town Hall was a two-storey Gothic building with a tower, surrounded by shops. After the 1748/49 fires Glaubitz rebuilt it, with S. Russel directing after 1756; the tower was finished in 1769: about 44 m tall on a 19.52 m square base, with a copper-clad wooden spire. It collapsed on 19 June 1781 during Gucevičius's attempt to straighten it. In 1785/86 Gucevičius drew three designs; the cheapest, without a tower, was chosen. Construction ran 1785/1788–1799 on the old walls and cellars. The magistrate spent over 22,000 złoty in 1788, then work stopped because of the 1793 partition, the 1794 uprising and 1795. A 50,000-rouble loan came in 1797; the building was completed in 1799, with interiors into the early 1800s. Its state in April 1794 (roof? portico?) is not confirmed. Square: main marketplace until the 19th c., with wooden and brick stalls, shops in the Town Hall and neighbouring warehouses, a day-labourers' hiring spot, a shame pole and gallows. Giuseppe de Sacco made a square-layout project in 1784, and Pietro de Rossi an enlargement project around 1799. The mid-19th-century fenced garden and fountain must be removed. 24 Apr 1794: the Act of Uprising was proclaimed on the square. 25 Apr 1794: Hetman Szymon Kossakowski was hanged publicly in front of the Town Hall, wearing a yellow dressing gown according to witnesses, while St Casimir's bells rang. Iconography: Gucevičius's 1785–86 façade and section drawings (Lithuanian Art Museum); M. Januszkiewicz's mid-19th-century watercolour of the Town Hall 'in 1763' (Lithuanian Art Museum).

- **St Johns' church, its bell tower and the University courtyards were essentially in their present late-baroque and early-classical form by 1794** [secondary-source]
  St Johns' church burned in 1737; Glaubitz rebuilt the façade (1738–1749). The bell tower (built 1600–1610, 68–69 m, the tallest in the Old Town) has three Renaissance lower tiers and two baroque upper tiers plus helmet added after 1737 to Glaubitz's design. The church's Pilies-street wall carried 1706–1710 plague frescoes and a crucifix inscribed '1706'. These were whitewashed under 19th-century Russian rule and only fragments survive, so the 1794 model should show them. Observatory: tower and upper floor begun 1753; south extension by Knackfus 1782–88 with a Doric frieze of zodiac signs (plus the Shield and Bull constellations honouring Sobieski and Poniatowski), under Rector Marcin Poczobutt (1780–99). Botanical garden of J. E. Gilibert in the Pilies 22 courtyard from 1782, moved by about 1797–99. The university then operated as the 'Main School of the Grand Duchy' (1781–1803; general knowledge, not re-checked here).

- **St Casimir's in 1794 had its baroque crown cupola, which dates from about 1755 and was later replaced (1864–68) and restored (1942)** [secondary-source]
  Construction started 12 May 1604; interior finished 1618. Damaged in 1655–61; after the 1706 and 1749 fires it was rebuilt by Tomasz Żebrowski, with the stepped lantern cupola and grand-ducal crown completed by 1755 (the church is 55 m tall). The Jesuit order was suppressed in 1773; the church became a parish church from 1799. Its use in 1794 is unverified. The adjoining Jesuit Professed House (1615) became barracks in 1798. Later history: French grain store in 1812; closed 1832; converted for Orthodox use 1836–40 by T. Tyszecki and transferred to the Orthodox church in 1841; rebuilt by N. Chagin 1864–68 as St Nicholas cathedral with onion domes and lowered towers; German Lutheran prayer house 1915; returned to the Jesuits 1917; partial restoration 1925; crown restored 1942; warehouse 1949; Museum of Atheism 1963/1966; reconsecrated 3 Mar 1991. The present exterior is broadly the post-1755 form.

- **Gates of Dawn in 1794: the gate and its 1715 brick chapel stood, but without the 1829 classicist façade; the pilgrim gallery was only just designed or under construction** [secondary-source]
  The gate was built 1503–22 (first mentioned 1514). After the 1610 fire the Gothic parapet was replaced by a Renaissance attic with arcade relief; the outer face shows griffins holding the Vytis shield and has gun openings. Chapel: wooden chapel 1669–71, burned 27 Apr 1715; brick chapel built c.1712–1715 and dedicated c.1720; stucco altar and interior decoration 1785–87. - **The city wall and all its gates were intact in 1794; demolition ran 1799–1804, and the Castle Gate went in 1837** [secondary-source]
  The wall was built 1503–1522: about 2.5 km long, up to 10–12 m high, of stone and brick, with an artillery bastion. Gates, with location and demolition date: Aušros/Medininkai (Gates of Dawn; survives); Rūdninkai, at Rūdninkų/Pylimo (1800); Trakai, at Trakų/Pylimo, the main gate with a chapel (1804); Vilnia/Vilija (1802); Tatar; Wet or St Mary Magdalene gate; Castle Gate (1837); Bernardine; Spas (1801); Subačius, at Bokšto/Subačiaus (27 May 1801). The Russian administration ordered the systematic demolition in 1799. The 1794 fighting damaged the Trakai and Spas gates. Survivors: the Gates of Dawn, the Artillery Bastion (restored 1966) and fragments near Maironio and Šv. Dvasios streets. At the south end of the spine the wall ran east and west from the Gates of Dawn, so it should be modelled continuous to Rūdninkai (west) and Subačius (east). Smuglewicz drew the Rūdninkai, Subačius, Trakai and Sharp (Gates of Dawn) gates in 1785–86.

- **Aušros Vartų street in 1794: St Theresa's, the Basilian Gate and Holy Trinity, and the Holy Spirit monastery stood broadly as now; no. 5 was the merchants' guest house, not the Philharmonic** [secondary-source]
  St Theresa's (nos. 12–14): monastery 1621–24, church 1633–54, consecrated 1652/54; after the 1760 fire the vaults were rebuilt in brick and a bell tower added under Glaubitz; Rococo interior frescoes by Maciej Słuszczański 1763–65; Pociej chapel 1783. Basilian Gate (nos. 7–9): 1761, Glaubitz, 17.9 m, three-storey late-baroque/rococo gate. Holy Trinity: founded 1514 by Konstanty Ostrogski; belonged to the Uniate Basilians 1608/09–1827; altered after a mid-18th-century fire; the monastery became a prison in the early 19th century (Mickiewicz 1823). Holy Spirit (no. 10): Orthodox brotherhood monastery (brotherhood from 1585, monastery 1609), which stayed Orthodox in 1794; church rebuilt by Glaubitz 1749–53 with a Rococo interior; Neo-Byzantine elements (dome, frescoes) added in the 19th century, to be stripped. Nos. 6–8: a Holy Spirit property with sgraffito after the 1653 fire. No. 5: merchants' guest house since 1503, rebuilt by J. Poussier in 1810, then 1899–1902 as the city hall and theatre (now Philharmonic). Its 1794 form is unknown and must be reconstructed.

- **The Jewish quarter lay west of the spine; a 1742 privilege barred Jewish residence and trade on the spine itself; the Vilna Gaon was alive in 1794** [secondary-source]

The Great Synagogue stood at Žydų g. 3: stone building of 1633 on a site in use since 1572; its floor was sunk below street level so it looked about three storeys outside but rose over five storeys inside. - **Population: roughly 17,500 around 1795–96, depressed by the uprising and siege; about 40% Jewish around 1800** [unverified]

The 1766 figure is implausible: Klovas et al. stress that pre-19th-century demographic figures are unreliable and put mid-17th-century Vilnius at only about 20,000. Jewish community: 3,887 in 1765 (city and suburbs) and 6,971 in 1800, out of a total of about 17,351, so roughly 40%. Before April 1794 there was a Russian garrison of about 2,000 against fewer than 400 Commonwealth soldiers. Gucevičius commanded the insurgent city guard of 1,500–2,000. - **Streets were stone-paved (fieldstone cobbles) with drainage channels; timber paving belongs to the 14th–16th centuries** [secondary-source]
  Streets had plank paving in the 14th–15th/16th centuries and stone from the 15th–16th centuries. Pilies street was 'paved with stones already from the 15th century'. Drainage channels existed on Pilies by 1642 and often clogged with mud. Paving stones were collected as a city entry toll; in 1791 the paving tax was 5 grosze per horse, 5 per horned beast and 3 per sheep. In the late 18th and early 19th centuries streets were paved with stone from the demolished walls. Archaeology at Cathedral Square found a 17th–18th-century paved square about 1.5 m below today's surface, and a late-19th-century street with kerb rows of larger stones and a sloped gutter. The Commissions of Good Order (boni ordinis), active in most Grand Duchy royal towns 1775–89, were responsible for paving and cleaning, but no Vilnius-specific record was found. - **Water came through gravity-fed wooden pipes from springs to public cisterns ('rurmusai'), including one on Town Hall Square and one by St Johns' bell tower** [secondary-source]
  Springs: Vingriai (1501, granted to the Dominicans; sold to the city in 1536); Žiupronys/Missionaries (1534–35); Gates of Dawn springs uphill beyond the gate (documented 1598). By 1648 a round brick reservoir tower stood between the Gates of Dawn and the Rūdninkai gate. Pipes were bored logs, from 1501 to the mid-19th century. Public cisterns ('rurmusai', from Polish rura, pipe) were wooden-lidded boxes or wells, called 'fountains' only in the 19th century. Locations: Lower Castle, Town Hall Square, Vokiečių (2nd half 16th c.), by St Johns' belfry (early 17th c.; shown in a Józef Oziębłowski lithograph of 1853). 1660: 15 private subscribers and 11 public-tap users; about 50 subscribers in 1677; decline after 1712; a 1738 voivode's decree ordered upkeep of the pipes. There were private wells (e.g. 1788). Metal pipes came 1878–89 and the modern system in 1914–16. Drainage: open channels (Pilies by 1642).

- **Roofs in 1794 were mostly steep clay tile; sheet metal only on churches, palaces and representative buildings; 19th-century roofs were lowered** [secondary-source]
  Archaeologist Daiva Luchtanienė identifies 8 clay-tile types in Vilnius: 'nun' (curved) tiles of the 16th–17th centuries; flat tiles of the late 17th–18th centuries; flat corrugated tiles of the 18th century. Glazes were red (most common), green, yellow, blue and lead-grey. Sheet metal was reserved for wealthy owners and religious or representative buildings; the poorest had reed or straw roofs. Fire rules inside the walls required brick buildings and tile roofs. Roof pitch (E. RISKS:
 * 'Today's building, back-dated' fails at the head of the spine. Cathedral Square did not exist in 1794: the Castle Gate, the Tribunal, the old Bishops' house, the chapter and vicars' houses and the Lower Castle walls stood there, and the ground was about 1.5 m lower. The wall and all its gates also stood. Those zones need geometry from the 1793/1798 Russian plans, the 1787 'French' castle plan and the Fürstenhoff-collection plan, not from modern map data.
 * Some present buildings date from after 1794 and have no 1794 shape to back-date to. Examples: Aušros Vartų 5 (Philharmonic 1899–1902; the merchants' house in 1794), the Presidential Palace (1824–34), St Nicholas and St Paraskeva (1865), the Chodkiewicz palace frontage (1825–34) and the WWII-era gap at Konstantino Sirvydo skveras. These need reconstruction from the 1808 plan plus conjecture, and must be flagged in the art bible.
 * Sources conflict on key dates: Cathedral roof statues (1792 per VLE vs after 1799 per Drėma, Smuglewicz 1797 and Peszka 1808); the Gates of Dawn gallery (1789–99 vs 1830–40); the icon's date and revetment (c.1620–30 with 1670–1730s revetment vs 1520–30 with late-18th-century revetment); street lighting (18th-century oil lamps vs early 1800s); St Casimir's Orthodox conversion (1832/1836–40 vs 1841). Pick one reading and record it, rather than silently choosing.
 * In 1794 the Cathedral (work halted 1792) and the Town Hall (work halted 1793–97) were unfinished building sites. Modelling them as finished neoclassical landmarks would be anachronistic. Their exact 1794 state (portico, roof, scaffolding) is not documented in the sources found.
 * Many findings rest on popular-history and institutional web pages (ldkistorija.lt, neakivaizdinisvilnius.lt, Wikipedia, lndm.lt exhibition texts, tourist sites) rather than peer-reviewed monographs. Population figures in particular are unreliable.
 * Access and licensing: the 1793–99 plans are in the Russian military archive in Moscow (fond 846), which is hard to access remotely. No shelfmark was found for the Fürstenhoff-collection plan in Berlin. The National Museum in Kraków holds the Smuglewicz originals and claims copyright on its photographs. Licensing for the VilniausDNR data is unknown.
 * With remote reference only, per-building storey counts, window types and colours in 1794 cannot be confirmed for most houses. Generic rules (2–3 storeys, 45° tile roofs, no balconies or shopfronts) will be wrong for some buildings.
 * Setting spine-facing Jewish shops or residences would contradict the 1742 privilege, which excluded Jews from the Gates of Dawn–Cathedral route. The Jewish quarter should read as adjacent side streets (Žydų, Stiklių, Mėsinių, Gaono).

This is the history layer for 'Shadows of Vilnius', with a real location for each event. I mined the earlier run's transcript and then ran new searches to fill the gaps.

**Main result.** Almost every key 1794 event happened on the spine or one block off it:
- **Signal and insurgent headquarters:** the Lower Castle arsenal beside Cathedral Square. A cannon shot at about 00:30 on 23 April started the rising, and church bells rang across the city.
- **Capture of the Russian commander, Maj.-Gen. Nikolai Arsenyev:** the Pac Palace, Didžioji g. 7.
- **Capture of Hetman Szymon Kossakowski:** the 'Müller house' on Vokiečių (German) Street.
- **Main night fight:** the Russian main guardhouse ('hauptwacht') beside St Casimir's, at the south end of Town Hall Square.
- **Town Hall Square:** the 'Act of Insurrection of the Lithuanian Nation' was proclaimed here on 24 April. Kossakowski was hanged in front of the Town Hall on 25 April, around noon to 2 p.m., in a yellow dressing gown, while St Casimir's bells rang.
- **Gates of Dawn:** fighting on 19–20 July and 11–12 August 1794. The chapel and its icon were damaged and repaired the same year.

The city fell on 12 August. Prince Nikolai Repnin was appointed governor-general on 30 October and arrived on 7 December. The Third Partition treaty followed on 24 October 1795 and King Stanisław August abdicated on 25 November 1795. The city walls were pulled down in 1799–1805; only the Gates of Dawn survived.

**Details worth building around:**
- **Calendar trap.** Russian sources use the old Julian calendar, 11 days behind ours. Tuchkov's '11/12 April' is our 22/23 April.
- **Easter week.** By my own calculation, Catholic and Orthodox Easter both fell on 20 April 1794. That puts the rising on the night after Easter Tuesday.
- **Darkness.** A Russian eyewitness, the artillery captain Sergei Tuchkov, wrote that the night was so dark you could not tell the colour of clothing three paces away.
- **Buildings mid-change in 1794.** The Town Hall had no tower after 1781 and was being rebuilt (1785–1799). Work on the Cathedral had stopped in 1792 for lack of money.
- **No street lighting.** Fixed oil lamps only arrived in the early 19th century, so people carried lanterns or torches.

**Factions.**
- **Russians:** 1786–96 'Potemkin' uniform. A black leather helmet with a crosswise crest, a short green jacket with red facings, loose trousers and cropped, unpowdered hair. Cossacks wore loose blue-and-red dress and carried red-painted lances.
- **Lithuanian army:** dark blue jacket with regimental facings, a crested leather cap and cornflower-blue trousers. The 7th Lithuanian Infantry had crimson facings and silver buttons.
- **Insurgents:** townspeople, clergy, students and Jews, armed partly with scythes and pikes.

**Guards at night by period:**
- Before 23 April: Russian musketeers (Narva and Pskov regiments) and Cossack patrols.
- 23 April–12 August: Lithuanian regulars plus a city guard set up on 1 May under the architect Laurynas Gucevičius.
- After 12 August: Russian troops again, plus a new police force with Russian officers and translators.

**Season.** For '2 a.m., October rain', late October 1794 in occupied Vilnius fits best:
- News of Kościuszko's capture on 10 October would have been arriving.
- Nights run roughly 18:40 to 04:45, and my calculation puts a new moon around 24–25 October.
- October averages 13.5 wet days and 85% humidity.
- Rain also makes flintlocks misfire, which suits stealth and blades.

Summer is poor for night stealth: there is no full darkness in late July, and 11–12 August was a full moon. My suggestion is to set the main story in autumn 1794 and play the April rising and the summer siege as memory missions.

**Sensitivity:**
- The Gates of Dawn chapel and the Cathedral are active places of worship. No combat or desecration inside, and never let the player damage the icon. A 1785 rule required everyone to walk bareheaded from the gate to the Pociej Chapel.
- The Jewish quarter is directly beside the spine. A 1783 ruling barred new Jewish residents from the spine route itself.
- The 'today's building, back-dated' method would wipe out the Great Synagogue and its courtyard of study houses. Both were destroyed after WWII; the site is now an active excavation whose future is disputed.
- Lithuanian, Polish, Belarusian and Russian readings of 1794 differ. Use 'Lithuanian army' or 'Commonwealth forces' rather than 'Polish army', and use both name forms for people and places.
- Do not make the player Kossakowski's executioner.

- **TOWN HALL SQUARE IN 1794: Town Hall without a tower and under reconstruction; gallows, pillory, shame post and market stalls on the square** [confirmed-reliable]
  The Town Hall tower collapsed on 19 June 1781 at about 15:30, while Gucevičius was trying to shore it up. It had leaned 1.7 m toward Vokiečių St; the building was 19.52 m square and the tower nearly 44 m high. Gucevičius's redesign drawings date from 1785–86, and the classicist building with its six-column Doric portico was finished only in 1799, after his death. So in 1794 the execution took place in front of a building mid-rebuild, with no tower and no Town Hall clock. How far the work had got is unknown.

The square had three permanent punishment structures (gallows, flogging pillory, shame post) and stalls; shops were also inside the Town Hall. Trade moved away in the late 18th century, and a tree-planted square with a fountain came later. The rebuilt Town Hall housed guard quarters, courts, the merchant guild, the treasury, a prison and nine shops, with a wine cellar.

- **GATES OF DAWN IN 1794: fortified gate and a Carmelite chapel fought over; chapel and icon damaged and repaired the same year; today's gallery and facade date from 1829** [secondary-source]
  The gate was built in 1503–14. A wooden Carmelite chapel was built in 1668–72 and burned in 1711–15; the brick chapel dates from about 1713–20, with a stucco altar added in 1785–87. The icon had already been damaged by a bullet in 1702, during the Swedish occupation. Polish sources say the chapel and image were damaged in the 1794 fighting and repaired that year; English Wikipedia cites a 1794 fire that needed repairs. The 1828–30 rebuild added the classicist street facade, gallery and stairs. Until 1844 the Carmelites controlled access, and the chapel opened to all only then. So a back-dated model must remove the 1829 gallery and facade. The walls were demolished 1799–1805; the gate was spared.

- **OCCUPATION TO PARTITION: Repnin governor-general, city law abolished, a new police with Russian officers, oath-taking in St John's and St Casimir's; Third Partition 1795** [secondary-source]
  Tuchkov says Knorring's corps stayed until mid-October 1794. Nikolai Repnin was appointed governor-general on 30 Oct 1794 and arrived on 7 Dec. On 14 Dec Vilnius lost its capital status. An oath to the empress was taken on 28 Dec: clergy in St John's (Šv. Jono g., the university church), magistrate and merchants in St Casimir's; the year is 1794 per Kurier Wileński, slightly ambiguous. The Russians abolished the city's Magdeburg charter and imposed the 1785 Russian city charter. They set up a police force of Russian officers, local staff and translators, plus a hearth tax and drink duties. The official 1796 population count was 17,451.

Wider war: Kościuszko was captured at Maciejowice on 10 Oct; Wawrzecki was supreme commander from 12 Oct. Praga fell on 4 Nov (Jasiński killed) and Warsaw capitulated; the end came at Radoszyce on 16–18 Nov. - **WEAPONS AND FIGHTING STYLES** [unverified]
  - Standard kit: flintlock muskets with socket bayonets, fired in volleys and followed by bayonet charges. Suvorov's maxim 'the bullet is a fool, the bayonet a fine fellow' is from 'The Science of Victory', written in 1795–96, just after.

- **JEWISH QUARTER AND COMMUNITY IN 1794: beside the spine; the community funded and helped defend the rising; the Gaon was alive** [secondary-source]
  The traditional Jewish streets were Žydų, Šv. Mykolo and Mėsinių. A 1783 ruling let Jews live in most of the city, except along the route from the Gates of Dawn to the Cathedral (i.e. the spine itself) and from the Trakai Gate to St John's; existing Jewish residents could stay.

The Great Synagogue and its courtyard ('Shulhoyf', Žydų g. 3–4) held about 12 study houses ('kloyzn'), including the Gaon's, plus the community offices and a bathhouse with ritual bath. The Vilna Gaon (1720–97) lived at Žydų g. 5; the house is lost.

In 1794, per Wirtualny Sztetl (via search), the community council gave 25,000 złoty, Jewish tailors sewed about 200 uniforms free, and Jews joined the defence. About 30 Jews were killed in a suburb by the incoming Russian army (JewishGen KehilaLinks page); the suburb is unnamed. The story that the Gaon's psalm stopped a Russian cannonball in the Great Synagogue is legend. The Vilna 'Pulver Purim' commemorates an 1804 explosion, not 1794. The Hasidic–Mitnagdic conflict (the Gaon's bans on Hasidim) was live at the time.

 * Calendar mix-ups: Russian memoirs and documents use Julian dates (11 days behind). Russian Wikipedia's 'night of 9 April' for Arsenyev and Tuchkov's 'July 13/31' are examples. Any date taken from a Russian source must be converted and cross-checked.
 * Many street-level details depend on one secondary source. These include the guardhouse fight, Nelepec at the Pac Palace, the Müller house and the prisoner counts (Sułek, via a 2019 Lithuanian translation excerpt), plus 15min and Kurier Wileński pieces. Verify them against the Polish original and the Lithuanian Institute of History's 'Vilniaus istorija' before scripting.
 * Sources conflict on key facts:
- Kossakowski's arrest date (23 vs 24 Apr).
- Execution time (noon vs about 14:00).
- Burial (St Stephen's cemetery vs Jonava crypt).
- Proclamation site (Town Hall Square vs Cathedral Square in the 1956 'Karys' article).
- Gallows site (Town Hall Square vs Pilies St, same source).
- City president (Tyzenhauz vs Morykoni).
- Date of the Council's dissolution (21 May vs 4 June vs 11–12 June).
- Tuchkov's identity (Sergei, per the 1908 memoir, vs 'N. A.' in the Russian Wikipedia uprising article).
 * Legends need labelling: the Carmelite who shot Col. Deev, the Gaon's cannonball, and Tuchkov's claim that he shattered the icon.
 * Mistakes from the automatic web summaries, all corrected: the Gates of Dawn icon called the 'Tuchkov icon'; Józef Kossakowski called Bishop of Samogitia (he was bishop of Inflanty); 'Pulver Purim' placed in 1794 (it was 1804); the 1812 'Vilnian National Guard' mistaken for a 1794 unit; Gucevičius 'wounded at Salaspils' (a garbled place name).
 * Level-art risk for the most important scene: the 1794 state of the Town Hall reconstruction and the exact footprint of the main guardhouse and gallows are unknown. The 'today's building' method would wrongly show the finished 1799 portico, the 1829 Gates of Dawn gallery, the finished Cathedral interior (1801), and no Lower Castle ruins, walls or gates.
 * The 'today's building, back-dated' rule erases destroyed Jewish sites (Great Synagogue, Shulhoyf, the Gaon's house). The site's future is under active public dispute in 2026, and misrepresenting it would be a serious harm.
 * Reception risk: active Catholic shrines, Holocaust memory, Polish–Lithuanian competition over Vilnius, and a Russia-as-enemy setting during the war in Ukraine. Uniform colours also differ between regulation and practice, and reenactor and museum audiences will notice errors.
 * The astronomy (Easter, moon phases, sunrise and sunset) and the season recommendation are my own calculations and judgement, not sourced facts.

A remote-only pipeline can back-date every spine building from evidence. Its core is the Lithuanian Cultural Heritage Register (KVR). It has an open bulk file (data.gov.lt dataset 2192, CC BY 4.0, 26,059 immovable objects, no addresses or coordinates). It also has a public ArcGIS REST layer (kvr.kpd.lt/arcgis/rest/services/KVR/pub_kvr_objektai) and an undocumented JSON service behind kvr.kpd.lt. For each object that service returns: dating and alteration chronology ('Century'), style, architects, 'valuable features' text (volume, storeys, roof form, facade, each with dated alteration notes), photo-survey lists and downloadable PDFs of the heritage board's evaluation acts. Modern geometry is free under CC BY 4.0 with National Land Service attribution (order D1-395, in force since 2025-01-01): orthophotos (the 1:2000 county-centre ortho, ≤10 cm), LiDAR (2017 county-centre set at >30 points/m², plus 2025–26 national scans) and GRPK footprints. Street-level imagery is the weak point:
- Mapillary is CC BY-SA, but its API needs a token, so spine coverage is unverified.
- KartaView has no photos within 300 m.
- Google cannot be used. The consumer terms bar copying; the geo guidelines say "You may not screenshot Street View imagery"; the platform terms bar tracing building outlines from Street View and deriving 3D models from 45-degree or photorealistic 3D imagery.

Back-dating cannot be a blanket rule. The register shows top storeys added in 1718, after 1749, 1775–90, 1800, 1881 and 1889, and in the 20th century, so each building needs its own evidence.

The method follows Assassin's Creed Unity (historians on the team, 150+ old plans studied, landmarks built at 1:1, a few deliberate anachronisms) and heritage practice: London Charter paradata, the Seville Principles, and the Apollonio 7+1-level uncertainty scale. It is a 10-step per-building dossier with per-feature confidence grades A/B/C and a separate gameplay-deviation layer. Footprints to ship in the game should come from GRPK (CC BY) rather than OSM, whose ODbL licence is share-alike.

- **Proposed repeatable per-building method (10 steps, remote only)** [unverified]
  1. Footprint seed: GRPK polygon (TOP_ID) as the master geometry, cross-walked to OSM ids and the address; add ID Vilnius NTRUNKNR where possible.
2. KVR join: spatial and address join against pub_kvr_objektai layers 0/1, giving code and GUID(s) and the parent complex.
3. Harvest from KVR: Century, Style, Author, ObjectFeatures, documents and attachments, photo list. Parse dated alteration notes into events (storey added, facade remodelled, balcony, shopfront, window change, roof change).
4. Read the acts: history narrative, 1790s owner, and archive call numbers. Queue remote scan orders (VRVA f.1019 PRPI studies and 1960s–70s measured drawings; LVIA; the heritage department's main dossiers; 1808 plan).
5. Georeference Kunicki 1805, Fürstenhoff 1737, the 1912 'late-18th-c.' plan and the 1944 ortho. Confirm the footprint existed in 1794 and the plot boundaries (plot data from Klovas et al. 2021).
6. Iconography sweep with historical street names on Commons, Polona, epaveldas/LIMIS and the national art museum's street pages. Log each image's date, view direction, holder, call number and rights.
7. Modern metrics from LiDAR: eave and ridge heights, roof planes, storey rows. Cross-check with Pastatai_3D, the ORT2LT/ID Vilnius orthos and the meshes (if licensed); Mapillary only if it has coverage.
8. Back-dating operations, each tied to evidence IDs: e.g. remove the post-1794 storey, restore steep tile roof, remove shopfront or balcony, restore window axes.
9. Grade every feature A/B/C and record paradata.
10. Separate gameplay layer (handholds, rooftop links, opened gateways, scale tweaks), each item flagged as a deviation. Historian review, then lock the dossier.

- **Rules for estimating 1794 heights when evidence is missing (proposal; numbers to be calibrated)** [unverified]
  R1. Measure the modern eave and ridge from LiDAR (Lidar_DR 2017, checked against the 2025–26 scans). Count storeys from facade window rows in KVR photos.
R2. Use any dated storey event in KVR or the acts: before 1794 keep the storey (e.g. Pilies 17, 1718); after 1794 remove it (e.g. Pilies 8, 1800). Grade A.
R3. Clues to a later top storey: a 'late 19th-c. / historicist' remodel on a 16th–17th-c. core; a top floor with different window rhythm or height; KVR phrases such as 'karnizas tarnauja kaip tarpaukštinė trauka' (the old eaves cornice now reads as a string course, e.g. Arklių g. 10). Then storeys_1794 = storeys_now - 1 (grade B).
R4. Default for main-street houses without clues: 2 storeys plus cellar and a steep tiled roof, 3 if the house is a palace or large corner house (grade C). Courtyard wings: 1–2 storeys, since the register often dates wing raising to the 19th c.
R5. Removed-storey height: the measured top floor-to-floor height, or else the median of A-graded neighbours on the same street (calibrate on the roughly 30 buildings with dated events).
R6. Roofs: steep gable or hip with ceramic tiles as the 1794 default (the post-fire tile rule is secondary-sourced; the pitch value is unverified). Ridge orientation and attics are taken from the 1805 plan, Smuglewicz, Wilczyński and early photos.
R7. Churches, monasteries, palaces, the Town Hall and the Gate of Dawn are always modelled from evidence, never from typology.
R8. Do not level the eaves line; the street was historically uneven.

RISKS:
 * The KVR JSON API is undocumented: it can change without notice (a KVR and KPEPIS consolidation project is under way) and its search endpoints reject scripted calls. Cache everything locally (already harvested to the scratchpad) and keep request rates low.
 * Rights for KVR photos, acts' appendices, ID Vilnius meshes and orthophotos, LIMIS and VU Library images are not stated. Treat them as reference-only until KPD and ID Vilnius confirm in writing; never redistribute or texture directly from them.
 * KVR 'valuable features' describe today's protected fabric, not the 1794 state. Older registrations (2003–2005) are thin, and iconography appendices are often offline, so some A grades will need paid archive scans.
 * Storey additions range from 1718 to the late 20th century. A blanket rule of 2 storeys in 1794 would wrongly shorten some buildings (e.g. Pilies 17, Dominikonų 15) and leave others too tall.
 * KVR objects do not map one-to-one to footprints: complexes, parts and multi-wing houses exist, and polygon centroids are territories, not buildings. Hand-check the joins for the roughly 100 frontage buildings.
 * Using Google Street View, Earth or 3D Tiles would breach Google's terms (screenshots, tracing and derived 3D models are all prohibited). Enforce this as a team rule.
 * Mapillary coverage is unknown and KartaView is empty. With field capture ruled out, facade texture detail depends on KVR photos (permission needed), Polona and Commons photos (PD but old and monochrome) and meshes (licence unknown).
 * Russian archives (RGVIA fond 846, the 1790s plans) are closed. The 'Atrastas Vilnius' publication is the only practical route to that material.
 * Shipping OSM-derived footprints could trigger ODbL share-alike; use GRPK.
 * The 1794 construction-site states of the Town Hall (Gucevičius rebuild 1786–1799) and the Cathedral force the same kind of recognisability-vs-accuracy anachronism decision that Ubisoft made for Notre-Dame.
