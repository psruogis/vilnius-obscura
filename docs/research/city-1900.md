# Vilnius around 1900 — research notes

Collected 2026-09-27/28. Tags as in `docs/REFERENCES.md`: **[V]** verified with a cited source,
**[U]** unverified or a design choice. Numbers in brackets point to the sources at the end.

## 1. Who lived here (1897 census, the city only)

Total **154,532**. [V] ¹ ²

| Native language | People | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Yiddish (Jews) | 61,847 | 40.0% |
| Polish | 47,795 | 30.9% |
| Russian | 30,967 | 20.0% |
| Belarusian | 6,514 | 4.2% |
| Lithuanian (incl. ~107 Samogitian) | 3,238 | 2.1% |
| Others (German, Tatar, Ukrainian, Latvian…; no source split them) | 4,171 | 2.7% |

Caveats:

- The census counted **native language**, not ethnicity. It undercounts families of Lithuanian descent
  who spoke Polish or Belarusian at home. [V] ¹
- The **Russian share is inflated**: military garrisons were counted as permanent inhabitants
  [V] ³. On the street, many of these people would be in uniform.
- Historians **criticise** the census: language stood in for ethnicity (and often reflected
  education or occupation), some speakers were assigned to nationalities arbitrarily, and
  Russification pressure pushed people towards "Russian". [V] ³
- The census listed Samogitian as a separate language. That is why sources give 3,131 or 3,238
  Lithuanians. [V] ¹ ² ⁴
- **Cross-check, 1916 German count:** Poles 50.1%, Jews 43.5%, Lithuanians 2.6%, Russians 1.5%.
  The Russians collapsed after the 1915 evacuation. The Jewish share (40–44%) and the Lithuanian share
  (2–3%) hold steady across both counts, so those are the most trustworthy numbers. [V] ¹
- Population about 162,600 by 1900, from one secondary count. [U]

**Small groups with a long history** [U, general history]: Germans (Vokiečių literally means "German
Street", and the Lutheran church stands on it), Lithuanian Tatars (Muslim, with a mosque in
Lukiškės), and Karaites (mostly in Trakai, with a small community in Vilnius).

## 2. The crowd mix for the game

A rough mix per 10 townsfolk c. 1900 [U, design, from §1]: **4 Jewish** (more near the square's
western edge, where the Jewish quarter begins: Vokiečių, Žydų, Stiklių, Mėsinių), **3 Polish-speaking**,
**2 Russian** (mostly uniformed: soldiers, officials, police), and **1 other** (Belarusian, Tatar,
German). A Lithuanian speaker is rare.

## 3. The shape of the city

- **The wall** was built 1503–1522, almost 2.5 km long, with ten gates (nine in some sources). It was
  demolished from 1799; the last gate came down in 1804. Only the **Gate of Dawn** and the artillery
  bastion survive. [V] ⁵ ⁶ Full list: `docs/gates.md`.
- **1875:** after a plan begun in the late 1860s, the tsar approved the expanded city. The New Town
  (Naujamiestis) spread west, and the new streets were given Russian imperial names on purpose.
  [V] ⁷
- **Wooden suburbs** joined in the 1890s–1900s: Šnipiškės, Naujininkai, Rasos, Žvejai, Naujieji
  Pastatai. [U, from a search summary; re-check]
- **Žvėrynas**, a resort sold off in plots by Vasiliy Martinson, was incorporated in **1901**. [V] ⁸
- The playable walk (110 m round the Town Hall) sits well inside the old walled city.

## 4. The Lithuanian community

- **No Lithuanian quarter was found.** About 3,200 people were spread across the city. [U: absence
  of evidence] Nothing was found on where ordinary servants and workers lived.
- **St Nicholas' Church** (Šv. Mikalojaus g. 4, about 262 m west of the Town Hall): petitions for a
  Lithuanian church began in May 1896. From about 1900, Lithuanians gathered in the neglected church to
  sing. It was handed to the community on **31 Dec 1901**, with **Juozapas Kukta** as priest. It was
  the only church in Vilnius with Lithuanian Mass until 1939. [V] ⁹ ¹⁰
- **The "Twelve Apostles of Vilnius"** (c. 1895; a priest, a nobleman and a forester among the
  founders) met in private flats. From 1900 they organised deliveries of Lithuanian publications from
  East Prussia and hid them in flats or **the attic of St Nicholas**. They staged three illegal
  amateur plays in 1900–02. The Okhrana had an informant report but did nothing. They became legal in
  1904: founding meeting at St Nicholas on 14 March 1904; Antanas Vileišis chairman, Kukta treasurer,
  Antanas Smetona secretary. [V] ¹¹

## 5. The ban, and other forbidden print

- **The press ban, 1864–1904:** Lithuanian in the Latin alphabet could not be printed, imported,
  distributed or possessed. Books were printed in Lithuania Minor (East Prussia) and smuggled mainly
  into Samogitia, Suvalkija and Aukštaitija. In the late 1890s 30,000–40,000 books a year came in,
  about a third of them seized. Punishments: fines, banishment, exile to Siberia. [V] ¹² ¹³
- **Vilnius enforced the ban.** Governor-General Muravyov (appointed 13 May 1863) ordered
  Cyrillic-only primers in 1864, and the Russification programme was drafted in the city. [V] ¹³
- After the ban, *Vilniaus žinios*, the first legal Lithuanian newspaper, appeared on
  **23 Dec 1904**. [V] ¹³
- **The Bund** was founded in secret in Vilna on 7 Oct 1897. A clandestine print shop opened that
  year and printed *Di Arbeter Shtime*. Yiddish itself was legal; the socialist content was not.
  [V] ¹⁴ ¹⁵

## 6. Landmarks

- **St Casimir's**, at the south end of the square, was rebuilt as the **Orthodox Cathedral of St
  Nicholas** in 1864–68: towers lowered, onion domes in place of the crown. It went back to the
  Catholics in 1919. [V] ¹⁶ `stcasimir.ts` already builds this c. 1900 front. In 1902, "St Nicholas"
  could mean this cathedral or the Lithuanian church 260 m west.
- **The Great Synagogue:** no downloadable model was found. The Sketchfab "Great Synagogue (digital
  reconstruction)" is **Erfurt**, not Vilna. Vilnius's own reconstruction (Historica Vilnius) was
  published only as video clips, and a 2017 VR version by Inlusion Netforms is proprietary. There have
  been excavations since 2011 (US, Israel, Lithuania), and the ruins were finally cleared in the 1950s.
  [V] ¹⁷ ¹⁸ ¹⁹ By the usual account, the floor was sunk below street level so the building wouldn't
  rise above the churches: modest outside, vast inside. [U, needs a source] Possible sources for
  drawings or data: Historica Vilnius and the Vilna Gaon Museum of Jewish History. [U]
